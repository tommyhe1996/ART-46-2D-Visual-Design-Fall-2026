Part 2 — What Is an Icon?

The class discussed McCloud’s idea of an icon.

An icon can be an image that represents:

* A person
* A place
* A thing
* An idea

The representation is not the actual thing itself.

For example, a drawing of a person is not literally the person. It is an image representing that person.

⸻

René Magritte — The Pipe

We looked at René Magritte’s famous pipe image from The Treachery of Images.

The painting includes the phrase:

“Ceci n’est pas une pipe.”

The point is that we are looking at an image of a pipe, not an actual pipe.

This helped connect McCloud’s idea of icons to a larger discussion of representation.

⸻

Part 3 — Simplification and Amplification

One important idea from the reading was that simplifying an image can sometimes make its meaning stronger.

A highly realistic face may look like one very specific person.

A simplified cartoon face may feel more universal.

Because there are fewer details, a viewer may be able to recognize themselves or other people in the image more easily.

⸻

Cartoon Faces

The class discussed how simple cartoon faces can communicate emotion very clearly.

Examples include:

* Smiles
* Frowns
* Pain
* Surprise
* Anger
* Fear

A hospital pain scale is one example.

The faces are simplified rather than realistic, but the emotional meanings are still easy to understand.

This shows that more visual detail does not always mean clearer communication.

⸻

Part 4 — Reality, Meaning, and Abstraction

We discussed a range between:

Reality → Simplified Meaning → Abstraction

Different levels of representation can create different experiences for the viewer.

A realistic image can describe a specific person or object.

A simplified image can emphasize meaning.

A highly abstract image can emphasize shape, color, form, rhythm, or concept.

⸻

Part 5 — Ways of Seeing

We watched part of John Berger’s Ways of Seeing.

The class discussed how seeing is influenced by:

* Habit
* Convention
* Culture
* Experience
* Context
* Reproduction

One major idea was that seeing is not completely neutral.

The way we understand an image can depend on where we see it, how it is presented, and what experiences we bring to it.

⸻

Images and Reproduction

Berger discussed how the camera changed the relationship between people and artworks.

Historically, someone might have needed to travel to a particular place to see an important painting.

With photography, film, television, books, and other forms of reproduction, images can travel to the viewer instead.

This can change:

* Context
* Meaning
* Value
* Scale
* How the viewer experiences the artwork

⸻

Part 6 — Connecting Berger and McCloud

The class connected John Berger’s ideas with Scott McCloud’s discussion of icons.

Both raise questions about how viewers interpret images.

Important questions included:

* What exactly am I looking at?
* Is this the object itself or a representation?
* How does visual style affect meaning?
* How does my experience affect what I see?
* How does context change an image?

This helped me understand that visual communication depends on both the artist and the viewer.

⸻

Part 7 — Abstraction & Meaning

We looked at different images or paintings representing similar subjects but using very different visual styles.

Some were more realistic.

Others were more abstract.

The different treatments created different emotional responses.

We also discussed Hilma af Klint and abstraction.

The lesson was that abstraction can be intentional and meaningful rather than simply decorative.

⸻

Key Idea

How something is drawn changes how we feel about it.

The same subject can communicate something very different depending on:

* Color
* Shape
* Detail
* Abstraction
* Composition
* Style

Part 9 — Quick Self-Portrait Exercise

We divided a page into four sections.

We created quick full-body drawings with approximately 3 minutes per drawing.

The goal was not perfection.

The purpose was to:

* Draw quickly
* Keep moving
* Make visual decisions
* Avoid overthinking
* Become more comfortable drawing the figure

Reference images could be used, but speed and experimentation were more important than producing a finished drawing.

⸻

Part 10 — Shape-to-Character Exercise

Another exercise involved creating random shapes using color.

We then used those shapes as the starting point for a character.

This exercise encouraged:

* Imagination
* Character invention
* Shape recognition
* Creative problem solving

Instead of starting with a planned character, we allowed the existing shapes to suggest what the character could become.

Part 12 — Layers

We reviewed layers.

The Background Layer acts like the paper.

It should normally stay locked.

Create a new layer above the background before drawing.

Important ideas:

* Each layer is separate.
* Layers can overlap.
* The eye icon controls visibility.
* Layer order changes what appears above or below something else.

⸻

Transparency

The checkerboard pattern in Photoshop represents transparency.

It means there is no background color in that area.

⸻

Part 13 — Brushes and Color

Brush Tool

The Brush tool can use different:

* Sizes
* Shapes
* Textures

These choices change the appearance of the line.

⸻

Color Swatches

Photoshop displays two main color swatches.

These function like a small working palette.

⸻

Eyedropper Tool

The Eyedropper tool lets us sample a color that already exists on the canvas.

This makes it easier to reuse colors consistently.

⸻

Part 14 — Paint Bucket / Color Fill

The Paint Bucket tool fills an enclosed area with color.

An important lesson:

The shape must be completely closed.

If the line has an opening, the color may spill into the surrounding area.

The Paint Bucket may be grouped with another tool, such as the Gradient tool.

Part 16 — Ivan Brunetti-Style Digital Self-Portrait

⸻

Basic Character Construction

The process included:

1. Large circle for the head
2. Simple half-cone or body shape
3. Noodle-like arms
4. Blob/simple hands
5. Simple shoes
6. Hair
7. Clothing
8. Personal details

The simplified style connects directly to the ideas from Understanding Comics.

A character does not need realistic detail to communicate identity.

Week 02 Key Learning

My biggest idea from Week 02 is:

An image does not need to look realistic to communicate clearly.

A simplified image can sometimes communicate an idea more directly than a detailed one.

Icons, cartoon faces, abstraction, and visual symbols all depend on the viewer recognizing meaning.

This connects comics to broader ideas in visual design.

⸻

My Visual Design Formula

Representation + Simplification + Abstraction + Context = Meaning

The artist chooses what information to include.

The viewer brings their own experiences and associations.

Meaning happens through the interaction between both.
