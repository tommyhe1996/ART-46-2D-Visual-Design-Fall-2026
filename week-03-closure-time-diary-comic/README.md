Part 3 — Chapter 3: Blood in the Gutter

Closure

Scott McCloud describes closure as the process of perceiving a complete idea from incomplete visual information.

In comics, readers often see only selected moments.

The reader mentally fills in what happens between those moments.

This means the reader becomes an active participant in the story.

⸻

The Gutter

The gutter is the space between panels.

But it also represents the unseen action that happens between panels.

For example:

Panel 1: A character decides to get a drink.

Panel 2: The character is already drinking it.

The comic does not need to show every step between these moments.

The reader fills in the missing information.

Part 4 — Reader Participation

Readers use:

* Experience
* Memory
* Habit
* Context
* Imagination

to understand what happens between panels.

This connects to earlier Week 02 discussions about how viewers interpret images through their own experience.

Part 5 — Say More or Say Less

The amount of information shown changes how much work the reader must do.

If the artist shows less, the reader may need to imagine more.

If the artist shows more, the reader may need to imagine less.

The important idea is that omission can become part of storytelling.

Part 6 — Six Panel Transitions

We discussed six major panel-transition types.

⸻

1. Moment-to-Moment

Very small changes happen between panels.

This can:

* Slow time
* Build suspense
* Emphasize facial expressions
* Show subtle movement

⸻

2. Action-to-Action

The same subject performs different actions.

Example:

* Prepare to jump
* Jump
* Land

This keeps the story moving efficiently.

⸻

3. Subject-to-Subject

The focus changes between different subjects in the same scene.

Example:

* One character speaks
* Another character reacts
* A third subject becomes important

The location may stay the same while the focus changes.

⸻

4. Scene-to-Scene

The comic changes:

* Time
* Location
* Characters
* Situation

This transition can compress a large amount of time.

Example:

“I want soda.”

Next panel:

The character is already drinking soda somewhere else.

⸻

5. Aspect-to-Aspect

The comic moves between different parts of a place, moment, or mood.

The story may not move forward very much.

Instead, the reader looks around the environment.

This can create:

* Atmosphere
* Mood
* Stillness
* Observation

The instructor described this like a wandering eye scanning a scene.

⸻

6. Non Sequitur

There is little or no obvious logical connection between the panels.

This may be used for:

* Experimental comics
* Humor
* Surrealism
* Unexpected visual relationships

⸻

Part 7 — Panel Size and Time

Panel size can affect how readers experience time.

A large panel may feel slower because the viewer spends more time looking at it.

A smaller panel may feel faster.

This means time in comics is not only controlled by the story.

It can also be controlled visually.

⸻

Part 8 — Comics vs. Moving Images

In film or animation, images appear one after another in time.

In comics, the reader may see:

* Past
* Present
* Future

on the same page at once.

The reader controls how quickly they move through the page.

This gives comics a different relationship with time.

⸻

Part 9 — Frame as a Visual Tool

We discussed how the frame itself can communicate meaning.

A frame does not always need to be a standard rectangle.

Panels can use:

* Rectangles
* Squares
* Irregular shapes
* Open boundaries
* Experimental arrangements

Panel shape can become part of the artist’s visual style.

⸻

Part 10 — Framing and Composition

Framing determines what the reader can see.

It affects:

* Focus
* Scale
* Distance
* Mood
* Importance

The frame can guide the viewer toward a specific subject or feeling.

⸻

Part 11 — Experimental Layouts

Non-uniform panel layouts can create energy and visual interest.

However, they can also become difficult to read.

Text, spacing, and visual flow can help guide the reader through more complex layouts.

⸻

Part 12 — Motion in Comics

Comics use still images, so motion must be suggested visually.

Methods discussed included:

* Motion lines
* Ghosted silhouettes
* Smearing
* Stretching
* Repeated forms
* “Zip ribbon” effects
* Position changes

Some of these ideas can also be borrowed from animation.

Part 14 — Photoshop Tutorial

This week introduced several new Photoshop tools.

⸻

Shape Tool

The Shape tool can create:

* Rectangles
* Ellipses
* Lines
* Stars
* Other shapes

For comic panels:

* Fill can be white
* Stroke can be black

Once satisfied, the shape may be rasterized before further drawing.

⸻

Layers

Continue creating new layers above the background layer.

Do not draw directly on the background.

Layers control:

* Visibility
* Stacking order
* Editing
* Organization

⸻

Crop Tool

The Crop tool can resize or reframe the image.

It works similarly to cropping a photo on a phone.

⸻

Transform Tool

Shortcut:

Command + T

Transform can:

* Move
* Resize
* Rotate
* Stretch
* Squish

Holding Shift can change how an element stretches.

Raster images can lose quality if enlarged too much.

⸻

Lasso Tool

The Lasso tool selects a specific area.

After selecting an area:

* Use Command + T to transform it.
* Use Command + D to deselect.

This allows one section of a drawing to be moved without moving the entire layer.

⸻

Brush Size

Brush size can be adjusted from the toolbar.

⸻

Help Menu

The Help menu can be used to find tools or shortcuts when I do not remember where something is located.

The comic can focus on:

* The whole day

* One small moment
* An hourly structure
* A personal observation

It is autobiographical, but I only need to share what I feel comfortable sharing.

⸻

Part 17 — My Diary Comic Strategy

For my own comic, I should keep the story simple.

A useful process could be:

1. Choose one part of Tuesday.
2. Identify 4–6 important moments.
3. Decide which panel transition works between each moment.
4. Sketch a simple page layout.
5. Decide where time should move quickly or slowly.
6. Draw the panels.
7. Add only the amount of information needed.
8. Let the reader use closure.
